## 2. Statutory Framework & Regulatory Boundaries

 

### 2.1 The Core Housing Benefit Regulations

The funding of rent service charges via public welfare budgets is strictly governed by national statutory instruments, rather than localized political opinion or flexible landlord policies. Local billing authorities are legally bound by two primary frameworks when evaluating claims:

*   **The Housing Benefit Regulations 2006 (S.I. 2006/213):** Applies to claimants of working age.

*   **The Housing Benefit (Persons who have attained the qualifying age for state pension credit) Regulations 2006 (S.I. 2006/214):** Governs older demographics where daily contact systems are heavily concentrated.

 

Under both sets of regulations, a service charge is only eligible for benefit coverage if payment is an express, non-optional condition of the tenancy and relates directly to the provision, maintenance, or essential structural management of "adequate accommodation."

 

### 2.2 Strict Analysis of Schedule 1 Exclusions

**Schedule 1, Part 1 of the Regulations** explicitly defines which service elements are statutorily **ineligible** and must be deducted from a claimant's eligible rent schedule. Daily safety check-ins and automated telephone contact triggers fail several distinct statutory tests:

 

*   **Paragraph 1(c) [The Emergency Alarm System Excluder]:** This clause explicitly bars public funding for "charges in respect of the provision of an emergency alarm system." Local authorities consistently apply this filter to daily check-in buttons, automated text prompts, or digital console screens, classifying them alongside traditional analogue pull-cords or pendant buttons because their primary function is to trigger an emergency response chain.

*   **Paragraph 1(f) [The Support Services Excluder]:** This clause prohibits "charges in respect of general counselling or of any other support services, whoever provides those services." Automated systems that require a resident to press a screen to verify safety—which triggers a human welfare call or landlord alert if missed—are legally classified as personal tenant support. This exclusion remains absolute whether the service is delivered by an on-site warden, a housing charity, or a private technology contractor.

*   **Paragraph 1(g) [The Accommodation Nexus Excluder]:** This acts as the structural baseline for local revenues and benefits officers, stating that any services "which are not connected with the provision of adequate accommodation" are entirely ineligible. Because automated daily contact does not fix physical wear-and-tear, clean communal hallways, or maintain the structural fabric of a building, it fails the accommodation test. It is treated as an operational cost tied to individual tenant well-being, rather than property maintenance.

 

### 2.3 Universal Credit Guidelines for Social Landlords

For claimants transitioning to the Universal Credit framework, the central government position matches the 2006 regulations. Under **Paragraph 20 of the DWP Universal Credit Guidance for Social Landlords**, eligible service charges are strictly restricted to four communal building categories (Category A: Maintained fabric; Category B: Communal cleaning; Category C: Communal lifts and entry systems; Category D: Eligible administration). The guidance explicitly places **"individual emergency alarm systems"** and **"counselling, medical or support services"** on the list of prohibited, un-fundable items.

## 3. Research Methodology & Data Collection Strategy

 

### 3.1 Empirical Research Design

This study utilizes a primary empirical research design based on statutory disclosure mechanisms to map out the administrative landscape of technology-assisted housing charges. To ensure an unchallengeable national dataset, primary data was gathered using the statutory mechanisms of the **Freedom of Information (FOI) Act 2000**. Because the requested datasets concern the allocation of public funds via statutory benefit frameworks, all responses obtained reside within the public domain.

 

### 3.2 Target Population & Multi-Tier Sample Mapping

The target population for this inquiry comprises all statutory entities responsible for administering housing welfare benefits within England. Sample mapping was executed across multiple tiers of government to ensure complete oversight:

 

*   **Tier 1: Local Billing Authorities (Borough, District, and City Councils):** These lower-tier authorities directly manage localized housing revenue accounts, process service charge line-items, and authorize payments. This group represents the core analytical sample.

*   **Tier 2: English County Councils (Upper-Tier Authorities):** Inquiries were dispatched to all English County Councils to verify administrative routing boundaries. Of these, **Durham County Council, Northumberland County Council, and Rutland County Council** operate as unitary authorities and directly retain benefit administration. Their formal responses are integrated directly into the primary results matrix. All other County Councils provided sign-posting confirming they manage Social Care budgets but hold zero statutory jurisdiction over Housing Benefit, proving this issue is processed strictly as a housing rent allowance matter.

*   **Tier 3: The Department for Work and Pensions (DWP):** To establish central legal compliance, an identical FOI request was served to the DWP Universal Credit Service to cross-reference central policy intent with local council execution.

 

### 3.3 Neutrality Safeguards and Submission Mechanics

To eliminate data collection bias, the data collection instrument was authored using voice-to-text dictation tools to guarantee absolute phrasing uniformity. The inquiry was framed to avoid leading the respondents, intentionally introducing the OKEachDay service as a representative example rather than the exclusive focus of the investigation. Every submission appended the explicit phrase **"or a similar service by a different provider"** to each question, forcing benefits departments to evaluate the fundamental operational characteristics of daily-contact service charges rather than focusing on a single brand.

## 4. Case Study & Representative Service Analysis

 

### 4.1 Corporate Profile & Deployment Infrastructure

The baseline model selected to evaluate the administration of tenant service charges is the **OKEachDay service**, which is frequently deployed as an independent checking tool or bundled alongside a broader landlord messaging framework titled the **Housing Proactive service**.

*   **System Developer & Provider:** Developed by Alertacall Ltd, based at 3rd Floor, The Base, Dallam Lane, Warrington, WA2 7NG.

*   **Verification Anchors:** Factual and operational verification is mapped directly via the primary Alertacall OKEachDay Service Hub and the Alertacall Housing Proactive Overview.

*   **Operational Intent:** The system is designed to provide automated or human-backed daily welfare verification, occupancy monitoring, and property engagement tools for older or higher-needs residents in social housing networks.

 

### 4.2 Legacy Hardware Execution (Pre-Upgrade Baseline)

Tenant records and case histories from the initial deployment window track a compact, physical daily-contact hub with highly specific user protocols:

*   **The Primary Check-In Anchor:** The resident was required to manually press a designated confirmation button on the physical unit before a specified local cut-off time each day to signal safety.

*   **The Contact Escalation Protocol:** If a user failed to signal safety by the target hour, an automated alert routed to the centralized Alertacall contact centre. Staff members would then initiate a two-way voice call using an integrated high-output speaker and microphone embedded in the baseline device.

*   **Landlord Trigger Handover:** If voice contact could not be established, the system triggered an immediate alert to the managing social landlord (e.g., Metropolitan Thames Valley Housing) to initiate emergency on-site welfare checks.

*   **Manual Hotlines:** The hardware featured a dedicated, secondary button that allowed the resident to bypass check-in rules and open a direct, manual two-way intercom call with contact centre teams.

 

### 4.3 Modern Hardware Evolution (The Digital Touchscreen Tablet)

To align with the UK digital telecare switchover, the provider replaced the legacy push-button hardware with an integrated, portable touchscreen tablet device:

*   **Integrated Connectivity:** The updated tablet operates on a wire-free, multi-network roaming 4G SIM configuration, ensuring communication stability even in properties lacking localized broadband or Wi-Fi networks.

*   **Digital Engagement Features:** In addition to the primary digital "OKEachDay" button and two-way voice call infrastructure, the system acts as a landlord communications portal. It displays text updates, maintenance notifications, safety graphics, and picture messages pushed out by housing providers.

*   **Resident Self-Service Options:** The touchscreen tablet includes interactive tools that allow tenants to author text notes, log voice replies, submit satisfaction surveys, and report property defects directly to their landlord's asset management database.

 

### 4.4 The Welfare Eligibility Dilemma

The modern features of the touchscreen tablet illustrate why this technology causes administrative conflict across English revenues and benefits departments:

 

[Touchscreen Feature Mix]
│
├─► Building Tools (Housing App, Repairs, Fire Safety Prompts) ──► Intensive Housing Management (Eligible)
│
└─► Personal Support (OKEachDay Button, Welfare Callbacks) ──────► Schedule 1 Prohibitions (Ineligible)

 

Features like the asset repairs app, fire safety alerts, and rent balance messaging tie directly into basic landlord operations, allowing some housing providers to argue that the fee represents an allowable Intensive Housing Management (IHM) cost. However, the core daily welfare call, the "I'm OK" button, and conversations designed to reduce social isolation fall under personal tenant support. Because these features do not maintain the physical building, they run into the strict exclusions of Schedule 1, Paragraph 1 of the Housing Benefit Regulations 2006.

## 5. Data Processing, Scoring, & Data Flow Chart 1 (Holds Data: YES)

 

### 5.1 Objective Scoring Framework

To convert qualitative, text-heavy legal disclosures into standardized data for evaluation, a rigid scoring matrix was applied across three distinct variables:

 

*   **Variable 1: Documentation and Data Status (Holds Data):** Tracks whether the local authority maintains official, searchable administrative records regarding these service charges within its benefits system (`YES`, `NO`, or `N/A`).

*   **Variable 2: Service Charge Eligibility Status (Allowed Status):** Records the formal stance or theoretical positioning of the authority regarding whether these check-in service charges are allowable under benefit rules (`YES`, `NO`, `AMBIGUOUS`, or `N/A`). For councils with no historic claims (`Holds Data: NO`), this field captured their prospective policy position if a hypothetical tenant claim were submitted, eliminating data gaps.

*   **Variable 3: Preferred or In-House Alternatives (Other Ineligible Service):** Logs whether the local authority operates a competing, duplicate, or preferred monitoring framework (such as an in-house community alarm, telecare, or tracking system) that could cause a conflict of interest (`YES` or `NO`).
